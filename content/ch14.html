At SDE2 level, the behavioral round often decides your level. Different companies give it different names:

  • Amazon: the "Bar Raiser" round.
  • Google: "Googleyness and leadership".
  • Everywhere: hiring-manager rounds.

If your technical rounds are strong but your stories are vague, you can be down-levelled — offered a lower level than you applied for.

14.1 STAR, done properly

STAR is a simple order for telling a work story: Situation, Task, Action, Result. The table shows how much time to spend on each part, and what each part must contain.

Part Share of answer What it must contain
Situation 10% The background in 1–2 sentences: the product, the scale, and why it mattered
Task 10% Your responsibility — the goal or the problem you had to solve
Action 60% What you did — your decisions, trade-offs, options you rejected, and how you convinced others. Say "I", not "we".
Result 20% The outcome in numbers, what you learned, and what you'd do differently next time

Notice that Action gets 60%. This is where the interviewer judges you. Most people spend too long on the Situation instead.

Keep each story to about 2 minutes. Then stop and let the interviewer ask questions.

Every story must survive three rounds of "why?" and "what exactly did you do?". If you can't answer the third follow-up, the story is not ready.

Rule: Tell only real stories, with real numbers you can defend. Interviewers are trained to dig into details. Made-up or inflated details fall apart by the third follow-up question. It is the same lesson as the "500+ problems" line on the resume.

14.4 The questions you will definitely get

"Tell me about yourself." (90 seconds. Practise it many times, but don't memorise it word for word — it should sound natural.)

Say this: "I'm a backend engineer with three-plus years at FieldAssist. It's a multi-tenant retail-execution SaaS used by 700+ consumer brands across 32 countries. I own the incentives, loyalty, Perfect Store and NOVA backends. NOVA is our agentic ordering assistant, used by about 190,000 retailers. The work I'm proudest of is making our loyalty workflows idempotent, so retries never double-post rewards. I also cut a critical API from around 30 seconds to 10. Our production stack is .NET, but I've used Java as my main language for problem-solving and design. I recently built a Spring Boot ledger service to go deep on the Java ecosystem. Now I'm looking for an SDE2 role where Java is the core stack and the scale problems are bigger."

"Why are you switching from .NET to Java?"

Say this: "The engineering problems I enjoy — distributed workflows, consistency, scale — are the same in both. The two runtimes are close cousins. Most product companies solving those problems at large scale run on the JVM. I already think and solve problems in Java, so I want my day job to match. I didn't want that to be just a claim, so I built [project] in Spring Boot with Kafka, Postgres and Testcontainers."

"Why do you want to leave?" Talk about what you are moving towards: bigger scale, deeper engineering, the Java ecosystem, and learning from strong peers. Never complain about your current employer.

"Why this company?" Give three reasons, all specific to them:

  • one product reason,
  • one engineering reason,
  • one personal connection to their domain.

Read their engineering blog before the interview to find these.

Other common questions — prepare a story for each:

  • your hardest bug;
  • a time you disagreed with your manager;
  • a time you missed a deadline;
  • a time you went above and beyond;
  • the most innovative thing you built;
  • a time you received critical feedback;
  • how you handle multiple priorities;
  • a time you influenced people without having authority over them;
  • a time you had to learn something quickly;
  • a time you simplified something.

Questions to ask them. Always have 3 ready. For example:

  • "What does a great first six months look like in this role?"
  • "What's the hardest technical problem the team is facing this year?"
  • "How do design decisions get made and reviewed here?"
  • "How is on-call structured?"

14.5 Resume rules for SDE2 product-company applications

  1. One page. Recruiters spend less than 10 seconds on the first look.
  2. Every bullet = action verb + what + how (the tech) + a measurable result. For example: "Built queue-driven idempotent accrual workflows (, unique keys) eliminating duplicate reward postings across ~ events/day." Fill the brackets only with facts you can verify.
  3. Only claims you can defend for 10 minutes. Remove "Solved 500+ DSA problems" now. Add a true count later — for example "Solved 250+ problems on LeetCode" once it is true. Your profile link will show the real number.
  4. Skills section: honest and in order. Put first the language you would code the interview in (Java). Then the rest. Add Spring Boot only after the project exists.
  5. Projects. Put the Java ledger project first under Projects. Include the stack, the concurrency guarantee, a load-test number, and a GitHub link with a good README.
  6. Fix inconsistencies. Dates must not overlap (right now, Senior starts Apr 2026 while the previous role "ends" Jun 2026). Remove duplicated bullets (the FlickPoints bullet appears twice).
  7. Keywords recruiters search for: Java, Spring Boot, microservices, Kafka, distributed systems, PostgreSQL, Redis, AWS/Azure, Kubernetes, system design.
  8. Export as a PDF with the file name Mayank_Shrivastava_SDE2_Java.pdf.

14.6 Getting interviews: referrals and recruiters

A referral gets you an interview several times more often than a cold application.

Referral message (LinkedIn, to an engineer at the company)

Hi <Name>, I'm a backend engineer at FieldAssist (multi-tenant retail SaaS, 700+ brands) working on
loyalty/incentive platforms and an agentic ordering assistant used by ~190K retailers. I'm applying for
<Role, job ID> at <Company>. I've built idempotent event-driven workflows and recently a Spring Boot +
Kafka ledger service (<GitHub link>). Would you be open to referring me? Happy to share my resume, and
no worries if not. Thanks!

Send 3 messages a day, starting from Week 13. Track them in a sheet with these columns: company, role, contact, date, status, next step.

Talking to recruiters:

  • Share your expected salary range only after you understand the level and the role.
  • Ask about the interview process — how many rounds, and what each one focuses on. Then prepare for exactly that.
  • If you need time to prepare, ask for 2–3 weeks before scheduling.

Offers and negotiation:

  • Try to get your offers at the same time. Plan your interviews so the final rounds fall close together.
  • Negotiate on total compensation, not just base pay. Use competing offers as your data.
  • Be polite and specific.
  • Notice periods in India are often 60–90 days, and this matters. Ask whether the company will buy out your notice period (pay your current employer so you can join sooner).
